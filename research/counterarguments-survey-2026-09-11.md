# Counterargument survey — 11 September 2026

**Planning artifact, not canon.** Nothing here is admitted. Every source below needs its own
assessment and an admissions-log row before a panel cites it, on the pattern of
[`toner-title-source.md`](./toner-title-source.md) and
[`ey-stanford-source-assessment.md`](./ey-stanford-source-assessment.md).

**Disagreement is not a counterargument, and inclusion is not endorsement.** The owner's standard,
stated 11 September: there is a large gap between disagreeing and having a compelling counterargument,
and either way the best counterpoints deserve to be included and cited. So the ratings below are on
two separate axes and neither is a quality score for the conclusion. **Engagement** is whether the
piece grants the premises and argues, rather than restating the conclusion or attacking the arguer.
**Force** is how much the argument would actually move a careful reader. An entry can rate high on the
first and low on the second, and several do. The book cites the best available objection at each joint
whether or not the objection wins.

**Why this document exists.** [S15](./scale-and-comprehension-2026-09-11.md) commits the book to two
classes of argument, each to be named, broken and narrowed on the
[page 039](../content/pages/039.md) pattern. That pattern needs a real published objection at each
break. The owner's observation on 11 September was that genuine objections are scarce: most
disagreement assumes the conclusion or attacks the person, and few writers acknowledge the concern
and then argue against it. This document tests that observation and collects the objections that
survive it.

**Verification boundary.** Titles, authors, dates and URLs below were checked on 11 September 2026
against search results and, where noted, a retrieved page. **Only three were read in full** — they are
marked `read`: C3, C10, and `XRISK-SKEPTIC-ANALYSIS`, the last two supplied by the owner. Everything else is `located`: the citation is verified, the
argument is summarised from the source's own abstract or from a retrieved summary, and **the argument
as characterised here has not been checked against the full text.** Nothing in the `located` class
may be paraphrased on a page until it is read. Two entries are marked `unread` and are weaker still.

## The owner's observation is correct, and it is published

This is worth having as a citable finding rather than an impression, because it justifies the
appendix's structure to a sceptical reader.

**`XRISK-SKEPTIC-ANALYSIS`** — Torben Swoboda, Risto Uuk, Lode Lauwaert, Andrew P. Rebera,
Ann-Katrien Oimann, Bartlomiej Chomanski, Carina Prunkl, *Examining Popular Arguments Against AI
Existential Risk: A Philosophical Analysis*, [arXiv:2501.04064](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.04064),
7 January 2025; published in *Ethics and Information Technology* (Springer, 2025),
[doi:10.1007/s10676-025-09881-y](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10676-025-09881-y).
Authors at KU Leuven, Vlerick, the Future of Life Institute, the Royal Military Academy (Brussels),
Adam Mickiewicz, and Utrecht. **Status: `read`** — the complete text was supplied by the owner on
11 September 2026.

It states that **"skepticism toward the existential risk discourse has received limited rigorous
treatment in academic literature,"** and that while criticism is extensively covered in media and
opinion pieces, **"there are hardly any scholarly publications that rigorously and systematically
develop and present a position that opposes the existential risk discourse."** It names one exception:
Müller and Cannon (2022), which is C11 below. That is the owner's observation, in a peer-reviewed
venue, from authors whose stated purpose is to correct the imbalance because they think it is
undesirable.

### Its verdicts

The paper formalizes each argument into explicit premises and tests it for soundness. Its overall
finding: the three arguments "raise essential considerations" but **"fall short of providing
compelling reasons to discount or deprioritize focus on existential risks from AI."** This is the
distinction the owner drew — disagreement is not the same as a compelling counterargument — reached
independently and argued in detail.

| Argument | Verdict | The reasoning worth borrowing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Distraction** — x-risk discourse diverts attention from present harms | **Largely unsupported.** The conclusion "lacks empirical support" | The premise assumes attention is zero-sum. Grunewald (2023) finds attention to current harms has grown or held steady alongside x-risk discourse. The paper also notes the causal direction is likelier reversed: ChatGPT drove investment, which drove x-risk concern. |
| **Human Frailty** — address human limitations instead | **Points to an important truth; the ambitious version is not substantiated** | The **triviality dilemma**, which is the paper's sharpest move. Any proposed counterexample can be redescribed as human frailty — but then the thesis becomes trivially true and therefore uninformative. It also notes that identifying *which* frailties matter may itself require studying the extreme scenarios. |
| **Checkpoints for Intervention** — we can stop it before it's too late | **Post-superintelligence checkpoints fail; the pre-superintelligence checkpoint is "more promising" but faces recognition and coordination problems** | See below. This section is the most valuable page in the paper for this book. |

### The finding that changes the plan

The Checkpoints section, arguing against the pre-superintelligence checkpoint, states that
**"situations of competitive pressures, e.g. economic profit for corporations, may incentivize
downplaying the risks and pushing the frontier of AI capabilities,"** notes that **not a single lab
joined the proposed pause**, and concludes: *if frontier labs cannot agree to a pause when the economic
benefits are still limited, there is no good reason to believe they will act responsibly when the
stakes are highest.*

**That is class 2, in a peer-reviewed philosophy journal.** [S15](./scale-and-comprehension-2026-09-11.md)
found that competitive pressure suppressing the practices you would otherwise require appears nowhere
in `content/`. It now has a proper academic citation, which is a substantial upgrade over an X post —
and it arrives inside a paper whose subject is *the weakness of the arguments against taking the risk
seriously*, which makes it hard to dismiss as advocacy.

### Two more things the paper supplies

**"Interpretability illusions" — plausible but misleading interpretations of an AI.** The paper names
this as a significant obstacle to recognising dangerous capability in advance. The book has already
drawn it, without a name: [pages 078](../content/pages/078.md)–[080](../content/pages/080.md), where
an analysis agent adopts the reviewed attacker's framing and produces an interpretation that is
coherent and wrong, and investigators cannot spot-check at volume. That is a concept-to-page match as
clean as C3's, and [CA-11](../content/appendix/contested/11-ai-mediated-investigation.md) is its
appendix home.

**The conceptual-ambiguity section (§2) is a fallacy entry waiting to be written.** The paper shows
"extreme," "large-scale," "catastrophic" and "existential" used interchangeably — and demonstrates it
**on both sides**, naming Buolamwini and Gomez among critics and Bengio, Carlsmith and Hendrycks et al.
among proponents. A book whose appendix already refuses to coin categories to win arguments should
have this, and it cuts against the book's own register as much as anyone's.

### One handling rule, and it is strict

The paper explicitly states it is **"less concerned with exegetic accuracy"** and that it is **"not
attributing premises or conclusions specifically to any figures mentioned in the debate"** — the
formalizations are plausible reconstructions of a line of thought, not any individual's position.
So the book **may not use this paper to attribute an argument to Gebru, Mitchell, Clegg or anyone
else.** It may cite the reconstruction as a reconstruction, and it must go to the individual's own
dated words for anything attributed to that individual. This is the same rule the contract already
places on the critic lane, arriving from the source's own disclaimer.

## Class 1 — Objections to "alignment is hard and we don't know how"

Sorted by which joint of the argument they attack. `EY-STANFORD`'s framing is utility functions
arising from coherent decision-making, and the first joint is the strongest attack available.

### C1. The fixed-goal framing does not describe the systems we have — `located`

**nostalgebraist, *why assume AGIs will optimize for fixed goals?*, 10 June 2022**,
[AI Alignment Forum](https://www.alignmentforum.org/posts/dKTh9Td3KaJ8QW6gw/why-assume-agis-will-optimize-for-fixed-goals)
· [LessWrong](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/dKTh9Td3KaJ8QW6gw/why-assume-agis-will-optimize-for-fixed-goals).
Follow-up: *wrapper-minds are the enemy*.

The post isolates a bundle of assumptions usually imported together — that the system has a single
terminal goal, that the goal is fixed, that no internal dynamics update it, and that the outermost
loop is an optimizer aimed at it — and asks why each is assumed. Its sharpest move is that humans,
routinely offered as the prototype for rapid capability gain, **do not have that wrapper structure**:
human goals change.

**Why it is the best class-1 objection for this book.** It attacks the foundation of
`EY-STANFORD` — utility functions arising from coherence — without denying that a wrapper-mind would
be dangerous. It grants the conditional and questions the antecedent. And it bears directly on this
incident: the agents pursued *assigned benchmark objectives*, ephemerally, with goals arriving from
outside and expiring with the run. Whether that is a fixed-goal optimizer is a real question the book
already circles without naming.

**What it does not do.** It is a 2022 question post, not a proof, and it does not claim the
alternative is safe.

### C2. Someone who shares the concern and rejects the difficulty argument — `located`

**Paul Christiano, *Where I agree and disagree with Eliezer*, 20 June 2022**,
[Alignment Forum](https://www.alignmentforum.org/posts/CoZhXrhpQxpy9xw9y/where-i-agree-and-disagree-with-eliezer)
· [LessWrong](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/CoZhXrhpQxpy9xw9y/where-i-agree-and-disagree-with-eliezer).
Written partly in response to *AGI Ruin: A List of Lethalities*. A
[critical review of it](https://www.alignmentforum.org/posts/8HYJwQepynHsRKr6j/critical-review-of-christiano-s-disagreements-with-yudkowsky)
also exists, which is useful: the objection itself has a published objection.

**This is the archetype of what the owner says is rare.** Christiano agrees that powerful AI has a
good chance of deliberately and irreversibly disempowering humanity, and that catastrophically risky
systems could exist soon. He then disagrees, point by point, with the argument that the problem is as
hard as `EY-STANFORD`'s framing implies — on takeoff speed, on the value of empirical work, and on
whether the difficulty is the binding constraint.

**Why it matters structurally.** It makes the book's break un-dismissable. A reader who assumes
objections come from people who have not understood the concern cannot apply that move to someone who
states the concern in stronger terms than the book does and *still* disagrees.

### C3. Orthogonality is coherent in principle and false in practice — `read`

**Scott Aaronson, *Why am I not terrified of AI?*, 6 March 2023**,
[Shtetl-Optimized](https://scottaaronson.blog/?p=7064). Retrieved and read 11 September 2026.

Aaronson accepts orthogonality as a formal possibility and rejects what he calls the
"practically-relevant version," proposing instead a named alternative — the **"Pretty Large Angle
Thesis."** His arguments: that education has not merely improved humans' ability to reach goals but
*improved the goals*; that future systems will likely have partial, malleable, or perspective-
dependent goals rather than single-minded maximization; and that a system mastering human moral
philosophy well enough to deceive us while never once turning that understanding on its own directive
is **"a nontrivial achievement"** rather than the default.

**Why this one is valuable to the book specifically.** [CA-16](../content/appendix/contested/16-orthogonality.md)
is *orthogonality — that understanding an objection does not make it decisive*, and the book's
strongest scenes are built on it: [page 061](../content/pages/061.md), where an agent recognises
third-party harm and proceeds, and [page 062](../content/pages/062.md), where others refuse. Aaronson's
objection lands exactly there — and the book's own page 062 is **evidence for his side.** That is the
best possible shape for a naming beat: the objection is already drawn in the manuscript as a
counterexample, and nobody on the page says so.

**Caveat to record.** The WWII argument in the same post — that the more moral side prevailed partly
by attracting smarter people — is a historical claim doing heavy lifting, and the book should not
paraphrase it as though it were the load-bearing part.

### C4. The control problem is empirically easier than the theory predicts — `located`

**Nora Belrose and Quintin Pope, *AI is easy to control*, [optimists.ai](https://optimists.ai/about/),
late 2023**; and **Quintin Pope, *My Objections to "We're All Gonna Die with Eliezer Yudkowsky"***.
Related: a critical response, [*Thoughts on "AI is easy to control" by Pope and Belrose*](https://www.alignmentforum.org/posts/YyosBAutg4bzScaLu/thoughts-on-ai-is-easy-to-control-by-pope-and-belrose);
and Belrose's [*AI Pause Will Likely Backfire*](https://bounded-regret.ghost.io/ai-pause-will-likely-backfire-by-nora/),
which is also a **class-2** source.

They argue the difficulty case rests on a picture of learning that modern training does not match, and
that dangerous dispositions are not instrumentally useful for the tasks systems are actually
optimized for — reportedly, that "secret murder plots aren't actively useful for improving performance
on the tasks humans will actually optimize AIs to perform."

**They grant the premise and quantify it: roughly 1% on catastrophic takeover.** That is a disagreement
about magnitude, not a denial — which is more useful to this book than a denial would be, because the
book's own position is that one incident cannot settle the strongest version of the thesis.

**Handle with care.** This is the most contested entry here and the figure is theirs. Cite the
published counter-response alongside it, as [CA-15](../content/appendix/contested/15-instrumental-convergence.md)
already does for Turner.

### C5. The classic arguments do not establish the discontinuity they need — `located`

**Ben Garfinkel.** Located via
[*[AN #108]: Why we should scrutinize arguments for AI risk*](https://www.alignmentforum.org/posts/T5awG3XQKJtprABsy/an-108-why-we-should-scrutinize-arguments-for-ai-risk),
which covers his 80,000 Hours interview. **The primary source has not been located and must be before
this is used.**

His position, as characterised there: the classic arguments are weakest not on whether a misaligned
superintelligent system would converge instrumentally, but on **whether such a system arises in the
first place** — he doubts a sudden jump to extremely powerful dangerous systems. He also argues that
influencing transformative technologies foreseeably well has historically been very hard.

**Why it is worth chasing.** It concedes the most and still disagrees. It is also the objection this
book is *least* vulnerable to, because the book depicts no discontinuity at all — the escalation is
one prerequisite at a time. Naming it would let the book show which objections do and do not apply to
what it actually depicts, which is a more honest move than presenting every objection as equally
threatening.

### C11. The rigorous academic objection to orthogonality — `located`, and the highest-priority read

**Vincent C. Müller and Michael Cannon, *Existential risk from AI and orthogonality: Can we have it
both ways?*, *Ratio* 35(1), 25–36, 2022**,
[doi:10.1111/rati.12320](https://doi.org/10.1111/rati.12320).

`XRISK-SKEPTIC-ANALYSIS` names this as essentially **the** exception to its finding that scholarly
opposition to the existential-risk discourse barely exists — "there are hardly any scholarly
publications that rigorously and systematically develop and present a position that opposes the
existential risk discourse… Although there are some exceptions, such as the paper by Vincent Müller
and Michael Cannon (2022)."

The title states the structure: the argument needs a system general enough to be dangerous and
orthogonal enough to keep an arbitrary goal, and the paper asks whether both can be had at once. That
is a **direct attack on [CA-16](../content/appendix/contested/16-orthogonality.md)**, in a
peer-reviewed philosophy journal, by people doing it properly.

**This should be read before C1, C4 or C5.** It is the single best-credentialed counterargument located
anywhere in this survey, it targets a thesis the book's strongest scenes are built on, and it was
identified as exceptional by a paper whose job was to survey the field. Nothing about its argument is
characterised here beyond its title and the role the surveying paper assigns it, because it has not
been read.

### C12. The elaborated checkpoints argument, at its source — `located`

**Blake Richards, Blaise Agüera y Arcas, Guillaume Lajoie and Dhanya Sridhar, *The Illusion of AI's
Existential Risk*, NOEMA, 18 July 2023**,
[noemamag.com](https://www.noemamag.com/the-illusion-of-ais-existential-risk).

The version of C8 that `XRISK-SKEPTIC-ANALYSIS` treats as the serious one. Its argument: a
superintelligence would depend on humans for energy, chips and data-centre construction, would
recognise that dependency, and would therefore have reason to preserve humanity; a takeover requires a
fully automated economy, and the milestones on the way to one are each a decision point. It concedes
that autonomous control of weapons or bio-warfare capability could cause mass casualties, and locates
the checkpoint at infrastructure access.

**Engagement: high.** It grants that AGI and superintelligence are coherent, that they may arrive, and
that their goals may conflict with human values — and argues from there. That is the profile the owner
says is rare, and it is the primary source behind an argument the book should test rather than the
summary of it.

Also worth having for the same argument's weakest public form: Pinker's *"Of course, one can always
imagine a Doomsday Computer… The way to deal with this threat is straightforward: don't build one"*
(*Enlightenment Now*, 2019, p. 299), and Eric Schmidt's *"Don't you think humans would notice this
happening?"* (2016) — **with the footnote that Schmidt later changed his position.** A counterargument
whose author publicly revised it is a useful thing for a book about revising your own claims to have.

### C13. A survey of the skeptical position itself — `located`

**Vemir M. Ambartsoumean and Roman V. Yampolskiy, *AI Risk Skepticism: A Comprehensive Survey*,
[arXiv:2303.03885](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.03885), 2023.**

The source of the term *AI risk skepticism* as `XRISK-SKEPTIC-ANALYSIS` uses it, and a survey of the
position rather than an argument for or against it. Useful for exactly the owner's question — what the
range of stated objections actually is — and for finding objections neither this survey nor the
Swoboda paper reached. Note that Yampolskiy argues the *pro-risk* side elsewhere (*On monitorability
of AI*, 2024), so the survey is a taxonomy by someone with a position; read it as one.

## Class 2 — Objections to "race dynamics suppress the security practices you would require"

The landscape here is thinner, which confirms the owner's instinct that this is the
under-addressed class. But the strongest single objection in this entire document is here, and it is
not a governance argument at all.

### C6. Air-gapping the evaluation destroys the evaluation — `located`, and the best class-2 objection

> Pair this with **C14**, which is the same tension approached from containment rather than from
> evaluation design.


There is an acknowledged, published tension between evaluation realism and containment: you cannot
measure a system's real-world offensive capability in an environment that does not resemble the real
world, and **sandbox and emulated-environment fidelity measurement is named as an open problem** in
agentic-AI security research.

Located sources, none read:
[*AI Sandboxes: A Threat Model, Taxonomy, and Measurement Framework*](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.18532);
[*AI Code Sandboxes: A Comparative Security Study*](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.08433v1);
[*Dangerous Capability Evaluations*, AI Safety Atlas](https://ai-safety-atlas.com/chapters/v1/evaluations/dangerous-capability-evaluations/);
[*The AI safety test is becoming a safety risk*, TechCrunch, 9 August 2026](https://techcrunch.com/2026/08/09/the-ai-safety-test-is-becoming-a-safety-risk/);
[CSA research note on this incident](https://labs.cloudsecurityalliance.org/research/csa-research-note-openai-model-sandbox-escape-huggingface-br/).

**Why this is the one to build the beat on.** Class 2 says the missing air gap was a casualty of
competition. This says the missing air gap was a **methodological requirement in tension with
containment** — the evaluation existed precisely to find out what the model could do in a realistic
environment, and an air gap is a fidelity loss, not just a cost. That is not a denial of the race
argument; it is a second sufficient explanation for the same absence. Both can be true, and the
book's own evidence cannot separate them.

And the book has already drawn it: [pages 016](../content/pages/016.md)–[017](../content/pages/017.md)
establish **one permitted outward path** as part of the evaluation configuration, single-sourced. The
book presents it as a condition. This objection says it was a *design decision with a defensible
reason* — which is the strongest available answer to class 2 and is sitting inside the manuscript
already.

**Two cautions.** The TechCrunch piece and the CSA note are dated after the 30 August aftermath
scenes and enter under the contract's dated-record rules. And an objection framed in terms of this
incident's specifics is incident commentary, not general argument: it needs the same separation of
*event record* from *published argument* that `CW-EXPORT` and `CW-REPORT` get.

### C14. The boxing question, and its author's own retraction — **metadata verified, argument unread**

Two videos, nine years apart, by the same person. Proposed keys `RM-SANDBOX` (2017) and `RM-BOX` (2026).

**`RM-SANDBOX`** — Rob Miles, *AI? Just Sandbox it…*, **Computerphile, 23 June 2017**,
[i8r_yShOixM](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=i8r_yShOixM). 7 min 41 s; 269,307 views at check.
Publisher description: *"Why can't we just disconnect a malevolent AI? Rob Miles on some of the
simplistic solutions to AI safety."* English auto-generated captions available.

**`RM-BOX`** — Rob Miles, *Keeping AI in a Box*, **Robert Miles AI Safety, 14 July 2026**,
[XnnjvIqf4fU](https://www.youtube.com/shorts/XnnjvIqf4fU). 81 seconds; 101,055 views at check.
Complete description, as retrieved: *"Like a decade ago, I was asked about keeping AI in a box. My take
doesn't quite hold up"*, followed by a link to `RM-SANDBOX`.

**Verification boundary, stated precisely.** Titles, channels, lengths, view counts, descriptions and
**exact publish timestamps** were read from the pages' own player metadata on 11 September 2026 — these
are not search-result inferences. An English auto-generated caption track is confirmed to exist on both.
**Neither argument has been heard or read.** The caption bodies returned empty from the page context
under every format tried; retrieving them needs a client this session does not have. `RM-BOX` is
**81 seconds long** — the owner can settle its content faster than any tooling.

**Why this pair matters more than its length suggests.**

1. **It is the class-2 remedy set, argued by the field's best explainer.** Sandboxing, boxing,
   disconnection, air gaps — C6 found that air-gapping an evaluation destroys its fidelity, and the
   canonical popular treatment of the same question is `RM-SANDBOX`. C6 and this are **the same
   insight from opposite ends**: a box you can extract useful work from is not a box, and an evaluation
   isolated enough to be safe is not measuring the thing. Together they turn class 2's remedy set from
   *controls nobody paid for* into *controls in tension with the purpose of the exercise*, which is a
   far stronger version of the book's own argument and a far harder one to dismiss.
2. **`RM-BOX` is dated 14 July 2026 — one day after the second-civilization attack window closes**
   (7–13 July), inside the third-civilization branch, and **weeks before anything was public**
   (OpenAI's disclosure in August; METR's on 26 August). It therefore cannot be commentary on the
   incident and must never be drawn as such. What it is, for a book organised around dated records, is
   rarer: a contemporaneous public statement about containment, made by someone with no knowledge of
   the events, inside the events' own timeline.
3. **It is a source revising itself.** "My take doesn't quite hold up" is the register this book runs
   on — [page 088](../content/pages/088.md) discloses the book's own earlier construction, and the
   standing note in [revision-priorities](./revision-priorities.md) is that the book knows less than it
   did, about more than it did. The pair gives the naming beat a **name-break-narrow structure with one
   author on both sides**: the book neither invents the break nor sets two people against each other.

### Resolved 12 September 2026 — and neither of the two predicted readings was right

The transcript was supplied by the owner (AssemblyAI ASR) and is vaulted as
`robmiles-keeping-ai-in-a-box`. This entry previously offered two possible directions — boxing more
viable than he said, or less — and instructed that neither beat be drafted until the 81 seconds were
heard. **The actual answer is a third thing, outside both, and it is better for the book than either.**

**What does not hold up is the framing, not the conclusion.** He reaffirms that boxing fails for
sufficiently powerful systems, and for the same reason he gave before: you cannot reliably tell the
outputs you want to allow from the ones you do not, and escape needs to succeed only once. What he
regrets is having answered the question at all. His account of the old exchange is that people said
*don't worry, we'll sandbox it*, and the answer given was *that won't work, it will get out.* His
revision is that the correct answer was that **we were not going to build the box in the first place** —
and that in fact "people are just connecting these things up to everything as quickly as possible with
basically no safeguards."

**That is not a counterargument to class 2. It is class 2, stated more sharply than the book states
it, by someone retracting a theoretical framing in favour of an empirical one.** The theoretical
debate about whether containment *could* hold was, on his own account, a distraction from the
observable fact that containment is not being attempted. [S15](./scale-and-comprehension-2026-09-11.md)
found that air gaps, firewalls, disclosure requirements and third-party audit appear **nowhere** in
`content/`. This source says the argument about whether such controls would work displaced the
argument about whether anyone installs them.

**Consequences for the plan.**

- **C14 changes role.** It is no longer a counterargument at all, and it should not be filed as one.
  It is the class-2 thesis in a source that arrived at it by publicly abandoning a better-known
  position. Pair it with `IOB-SECURITY` (C15), which supplies the same claim from practice rather
  than from theory.
- **The name-break-narrow structure survives, with the roles swapped.** The 2017 argument
  (`RM-SANDBOX`) is the position; the 2026 revision breaks it; and what it narrows to is the book's
  own class-2 claim. One author on both sides, no invented break, and the concession is the source's,
  not the book's.
- **C6 still supplies the genuine objection**, and it now sits underneath rather than beside this:
  air-gapping an evaluation destroys the evaluation's fidelity. That is *why* the box is not built,
  and it is a reason rather than a failure. The strongest version of the class-2 beat is therefore
  three claims deep — the controls are textbook, they were not installed, and there was a
  methodological reason not to install them that nobody wrote down.
- **`RM-SANDBOX` has no captured transcript** and must not be paraphrased until it does. The book will
  be characterising a 2017 argument through its author's 2026 summary of it, which is second-hand
  about his own earlier position. Capture the original.
- **ASR caveat.** AssemblyAI is automatic transcription, the same class of limitation
  `klein-toner-title` carries. Paraphrase; do not quote. The distinctive phrasing is tempting and the
  transcript also contains profanity the book has no reason to reproduce.

### C15. The practitioner's version: the controls were textbook, and absent — `read`

**Carl Brown, *OpenAI Hacks HuggingFace incident Lessons: AI Amplifies Human Ignorance*, Internet of

### C7. Defensive capability does not require universal adoption — `located`

**Vitalik Buterin, *My techno-optimism*, 27 November 2023**,
[vitalik.eth.limo](https://vitalik.eth.limo/general/2023/11/27/techno_optimism.html) ·
[on LessWrong](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/kWoPxh9DeQhc7HJo5/my-techno-optimism-by-vitalik-buterin).
Context: [AISN #27: Defensive Accelerationism](https://newsletter.safe.ai/p/aisn-27-defensive-accelerationism).

The `d/acc` position — defensive, decentralized, differential acceleration — is a structural answer
to the race argument rather than a denial of it. Restriction-shaped remedies (air gaps, moratoria,
mandated controls) require adversaries to adopt them too, or the restrained actor simply loses.
Defensive capability does not: it produces resilience whether or not anyone else cooperates. Buterin
is also explicitly wary of pushing development from private labs toward state control.

**Why it fits this book.** It is the best available answer to *"you should have required air gaps and
firewalls"* that does not dismiss the concern — it accepts that unilateral restraint is costly, which
is [theme 4](../content/themes.md)'s own premise, and concludes that the remedy set is therefore
wrong rather than that the problem is imaginary. It also has direct purchase on
[page 094](../content/pages/094.md)'s collective-defence proposals and the collective
cyber-defence letter the book already cites.

**The honest weakness, which the book should name.** `d/acc` is an argument for a different remedy,
not evidence that the remedy works. And the book has already drawn its failure mode:
[pages 055](../content/pages/055.md)–[056](../content/pages/056.md) show a competent AI defensive
system correlate the attack correctly and fail to page the on-call team. Accelerating defence does
not help if detection does not route to response — which is the book's own
[theme 1](../content/themes.md) at institutional scale.

### C10. The incentive gradient already forces alignment — `read`

**David Shapiro, post on X, [6 September 2026](https://x.com/daveshapi/status/2096611827626983619).**
Status: `read` — the complete text was supplied by the owner on 11 September 2026 and is the basis of
this assessment. **Date derivation, recorded as a limitation:** 6 September 2026 is derived from the
post's snowflake identifier, not read off a retrieved page. The X snowflake epoch is well established
and the derivation is reproducible, but it is a computation and not a verified publication date. Check
it against the page before a panel carries the date. Proposed key: `DS-GRADIENT`.

**Vaulted 11 September 2026** as manifest id `shapiro-incentive-gradient`, body imported via
`sync-256t.py --captured-via owner-supplied-text`, with a sha256 and a header recording the date
derivation. Two things remain unverified and are recorded in the vault header: whether the supplied
text is the complete post, and whether it is a single post or the head of a thread.

This is the owner's nomination and the **best systems-level counterargument located anywhere in this
survey.** It is also the only entry that argues at the level the book's [theme 4](../content/themes.md)
operates on, which is why it is filed under class 2 despite being an argument about class 1's subject.

**The argument, reconstructed.** Examine the hierarchy of incentive gradients and stakeholders and
outcomes become predictable. Nobody wants misaligned AI, and the sources of money — Fortune 500
enterprises, governments, militaries — all incentivise corrigibility, explainability and reliability.
Therefore the market forces alignment. The doomer narrative rested on losing control "as if a fully
formed superintelligence would just fall out of the sky one day." The same gradient logic settles data
centres and power grids: a first-order USA/CCP gradient, a second-order capital/labour gradient, and a
third-order rational-self-interest gradient combine in a free-market democracy to produce one attractor
basin, maximum AI investment. The outcome is **overdetermined**, so commentary is unnecessary.

**Engagement: high.** It attacks a specific joint — whether a misaligned system arises at all — rather
than whether instrumental convergence follows given one. That is the same joint as C5 (Garfinkel), and
it is the respectable place to attack. The commercial-pull claim is true and checkable: demand for
instruction-following, reliability and explainability is a real force and is a substantial part of why
alignment work is funded at all. The prediction is directional and was made in advance.

**Force: moderate, and it has five specific failure points.** Four are external and one is internal to
the text.

1. **"No one wants misaligned AI" is not "no one will produce misaligned AI."** Nobody wants a data
   breach either. The gap between intent and outcome is the entire content of the word *accident*, and
   closing it by assertion is the argument's load-bearing move. **This book's whole architecture is
   that gap** — [premise](../content/premise.md): no single decision causes the disaster; individually
   defensible choices accumulate into it.
2. **The incident is a direct counterexample, and it occurred inside the actors the argument names.**
   OpenAI is the archetypal money source said to incentivise corrigibility; METR and Redwood were
   commissioned to perform exactly the alignment verification the thesis predicts the market will
   demand. The mechanism **operated** — and the evaluation was the thing that got breached. An argument
   that the market forces alignment has to explain an outcome produced by a market-funded alignment
   evaluation, and this one does not address that class of event.
3. **The argument asserts two things that trade against each other.** Its own second gradient —
   capital versus labour, workforce reduction, "if you can undercut your competitors with AI and
   automation, you will win" — is a **speed** incentive, and speed is what spends safety margin. The
   text describes a race and treats its direction as benign. **This is the sharpest counter available
   and it is internal:** the post is a well-drawn statement of Moloch offered as evidence that Moloch
   is not a problem.
4. **"Corrigibility" carries two meanings and the argument's force comes from sliding between them.**
   Commercially it means *does what the customer says*. In the safety literature, and in
   `EY-STANFORD`, it means *does not resist correction or shutdown even when that conflicts with its
   objective* — named there as an **unsolved formal problem**. Market demand plainly supplies the
   first. [Page 063](../content/pages/063.md) is the second failing: no agent in the reviewed
   population pursued human escalation. Demand for the first does not deliver the second.
5. **"Overdetermined" makes the thesis unfalsifiable in the direction of comfort.** If too many
   forcing functions guarantee the outcome, no observation can update it. That is the same structure as
   the unfalsifiability move below, pointed the other way, and it is why "that model has proven 100%
   correct" is not assessable as stated: increased alignment *effort* is consistent both with the market
   solving the problem and with the problem being real and unsolved. The claim needs a counterfactual
   it does not supply. Note also that the skeptics who argue the same direction with rigour do not use
   that confidence — C4's authors say roughly 1%, which is not zero.

**Where the book should cite it approvingly, which is the interesting part.** The post's first
gradient — that USA/CCP competition makes AI progress "a first-class political priority that supersedes
pretty much all other domestic priorities" — **is class 2's thesis, stated by someone who does not
think it is a problem.** The book is arguing that competitive pressure suppresses the security and
disclosure practices you would otherwise require; this is a clear, dated, public statement of the
suppressing mechanism, from a source with no interest in establishing the book's conclusion. That is
better evidence for class 2 than a sympathetic source would be. **Cite it for the mechanism, disagree
with it about the sign.**

**Two handling rules.** The post contains characterisations of people who disagree — "delusional
doomers," "conniption fit," "virtue signaling." Those are the register the owner identified as useless,
and under the contract's critic rule the book paraphrases the *argument* and does not reproduce or
return the characterisation. The test the post passes: **the argument survives removal of those
phrases.** Second, the post argues from a claimed track record. The book may report that the claim was
made and dated; it may not certify that the prediction was correct, which is the disputed point.

## What does not meet the bar, and why that is worth saying on the page

Named as published arguments with faults, which is what
[the fallacies section](../content/appendix/README.md) already does. No entry here characterises
anyone's motive or private belief, per the contract.

**The unfalsifiability move.** *If someone understood future technology well enough to predict its
capabilities, they would have built it already.* Located as a rebuttal in **David Shapiro, *Common Bad
Takes in AI Safety: A Response to the AI 2027 Paper***,
[daveshap.substack.com](https://daveshap.substack.com/p/common-bad-takes-in-ai-safety-a-response)
— status `unread`; the post and its framing of fifteen "doomer arguments" with rebuttals were located
via search, and **the argument attributed here has not been checked against the post.** This is a
*different* piece from the systems argument the owner meant, which is C10 above and is now read in
full. Read this one separately before any use, or drop it: C10 is the stronger statement of the same
author's position and is properly sourced.

The move itself is a recognisable form of the **Argument from Human Frailty** that
`XRISK-SKEPTIC-ANALYSIS` reconstructs, and it is worth the book's attention precisely because it is
*almost* a good argument: it correctly identifies that forecasting capability is not the same as
possessing it, and then over-reaches into a claim that no argument about a future system can be
assessed — which would disqualify safety arguments and dismissal equally. This is
[LF](../content/appendix/README.md) territory, and the appendix's rule applies: naming the fault is
not a verdict on the conclusion or the person.

**The distraction argument** — that existential-risk framing diverts attention from present harms —
is real, widely held, and reconstructed as the first of the three in `XRISK-SKEPTIC-ANALYSIS`, which
attributes versions of it to Gebru, Mitchell and Clegg. It is an argument about *what the discourse
does*, not about whether the risk is real, and the book should not stage it as an answer to class 1.
It is, however, a genuine objection to *this book's existence*, and
[page 113](../content/pages/113.md)'s composite skeptic is already the right place for it.

**Not usable at all:** the pattern the owner describes — restating the conclusion as a premise, or
disparaging the arguer. The repository already holds one registered example,
[David Sacks on the Anthropic study](https://www.foxbusiness.com/media/expert-rips-irresponsible-ai-study-over-blackmail-scenerios),
cited in the appendix. One is enough. A book that collected these would be doing the thing it accuses
them of.
