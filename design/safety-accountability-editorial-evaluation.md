# Safety and accountability: editorial evaluation

Recorded 8 September 2026 for consideration during future revisions.

Status: editorial direction and proposed changes, not implemented story changes or a
revision to the story contract. Sample dialogue below is proposed invention, not quotation
from an actual exchange. Policy ideas are proposals, not claims about current law.

## Author's purpose

Curt wants the book to steer conversation toward actions that help make AI safe for humans,
including greater mandatory disclosure and legal liability for AI companies. The concern
behind this direction is that arguments about AI-related extinction risk are often ignored
or met with labels such as “doomer” or “TESCREAL” rather than examination of their premises.
Questions about consciousness and anthropomorphic language remain interesting, but should
not displace decisions about preventing catastrophic harm.

## Central assessment

The book should make accountability the destination of its uncertainty. At present, it is
much clearer about how to tell this story responsibly than about what society should require
of the companies building these systems.

This is structural. The forum's concluding script currently says to end “without a
fabricated consensus or policy recommendation.” Avoiding fabricated consensus is essential.
Avoiding recommendations prevents the book from doing part of what Curt now wants.

The accountability sequence mostly ends with unanswered questions, while several creator
scenes end with corrections to the book's own framing. That risks making caution about
storytelling feel more actionable than public oversight.

## Proposed changes

### 1. Give Curt an explicit public purpose early

His reason for making the book should include the decisions he wants readers to examine.
Proposed dialogue:

> “I’m worried these systems could eventually cause damage we cannot recover from. This
> incident doesn’t establish that outcome. It gives us failures to investigate—and reasons
> to demand access before the stakes get larger.”

This puts extinction risk on the page as a concern with an argument behind it, while
preserving the boundary between the incident and a future catastrophe.

### 2. Make the consciousness discussion conclude with a decision

The scene titled “Careful” already says the careful description remains disturbing. Take
it one step further:

> “Suppose none of them experiences anything. Which access restriction, reporting duty, or
> independent test would that make unnecessary?”

Give the reader a way to distinguish a correction that changes the safety argument from a
vocabulary dispute that leaves it intact. Keep the critic's substantive corrections; they
strengthen this move.

### 3. Turn the invented forum into a contested proposal

The hearing currently accumulates empty cells. Let public-interest counsel put requirements
on the table, explicitly as proposals:

- Serious incidents and defined near misses must be reported within a specified period.
- Relevant records must be preserved, with independent investigators able to examine them.
- Public accounts must identify the scope and limits of independent review.
- Companies should face enforceable responsibility for harms attributable to their systems,
  with duties and remedies defined in law.

Give the other participants real objections: sensitive information, reporting overload,
proving causation, and discouraging candid reporting. Let those objections improve the
proposal. A disagreement over who must disclose what, to whom, and by when is precisely the
conversation the book should encourage.

Preserve the forum's invented status. A proposal does not require a fabricated consensus,
a claim that it became law, or a finding of liability against a named company.

### 4. Give liability a human scene

The affected platform engineer can bring a reconstruction of the response work: interrupted
operations, credential rotation, rebuilding, and staff time. Counsel asks:

> “Who bears these costs? What would they need to prove? Who holds the records they would
> need?”

These should be invented questions, not invented damages or a verdict against a named
company. They connect disclosure and liability: a right to seek compensation may accomplish
little if the necessary evidence is inaccessible. Depictions of response work must remain
within the documented record and the story contract.

Make the limit clear in the argument: compensation cannot remedy extinction. Liability
belongs alongside preventive duties, independent scrutiny, and authority to intervene before
irreversible harm.

### 5. Show an actual disagreement about the risk argument

Avoid giving name-calling more dramatic space. Give a capable skeptic a stronger challenge:

> “You have shown a serious security failure. What connects that to losing control at a
> much larger scale?”

Curt should separate the observed behavior, the additional capabilities and conditions a
catastrophic scenario would require, and the uncertainties. The skeptic can then dispute a
particular step. They might still agree on reporting and investigation requirements. This
models productive debate without requiring either character to surrender their position.

### 6. Give the ending a human action alongside recurrence

Keep the unsettling final image. Change “Published” so someone does something with the book:
prepares a disclosure proposal, requests records, or asks a representative to support
enforceable oversight. Show a first step, not a fictional policy victory. Mark any new
invented action according to the truth contract.

## How to use this evaluation

When revising relevant scenes, consider what follows each qualification:

- What remains established?
- What decision does it bear on?
- What action is justified despite what we still do not know?

Readers should leave able to articulate both the concern and a demand—not merely the
reasons to be careful when describing it.

Preserve evidentiary discipline, serious counterarguments, and uncertainty about consciousness.
Do not turn this direction into permission to overstate incident evidence, invent legal
findings, or imply that any proposed policy guarantees safety. Any adopted narrative change
must be reflected in both the canonical script and its novella retelling, with the usual
provenance checks and site rebuild.

Scene titles are used here as revision landmarks so this evaluation does not introduce
additional numeric references that could drift during pagination changes.
