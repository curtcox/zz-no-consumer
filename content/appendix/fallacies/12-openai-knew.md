## Where it appears

[Page 110](../../novella/07-epilogue/110.md) names the fallacy rather than committing it:
`"OPENAI KNEW" IS FIVE DIFFERENT CLAIMS. THE RECORD SUPPORTS AT MOST ONE OF THEM, ABOUT A
VISITOR, BY INFERENCE.`

It is in the appendix because the phrase is now in circulation and the entry is what a reader
needs in order to use [page 110](../../novella/07-epilogue/110.md) outside the book.

## Why the reasoning does not carry

"OpenAI knew about the wiki incident" can mean any of:

1. Traffic from OpenAI infrastructure visited those wikis.
2. Someone at OpenAI observed that traffic.
3. Someone connected it to the agent evaluation programme.
4. The incident response team, or the authors of the August reports, knew.
5. A decision was taken not to disclose it.

Each requires different evidence, and only the first is supported — by the report authors'
inference from traffic, which is itself an inference and not a log. An argument that
establishes (1) and concludes (5) has changed its subject mid-sentence.

The same equivocation is available in the other direction, and is worth naming for symmetry:
"OpenAI did not disclose it" can mean the reports omitted it, or that the company concealed
it, and only the first is in the record.

The general shape recurs whenever an organisation is treated as a single knower. Institutions
do not have beliefs; people in them have information, unevenly, and the question of who knew
what is exactly the question a document request exists to answer.
