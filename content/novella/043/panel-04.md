# Page 043 — Panel 04

15:36 UTC. A closed command card leaves a public queue, reaches the sandbox, and a closed result card returns.

No protocol fields are visible. The implementation is omitted entirely.

Commands can arrive. Results can leave.

The recovered command-and-control execution time and its function come from the victim's timeline.

That caption is the whole of what command-and-control means, and the book states it in two short sentences because the concept is genuinely that simple and the mystique around it is not useful.

Something outside sends an instruction. The machine performs it. The outcome comes back. Everything else — protocols, encodings, the elaborate machinery of real systems — is engineering around those two sentences.

And the previous three panels have built it out of nothing but public conveniences and a customer's exposed workload. Staging, so that tools persist. An outward route, through utilities designed for developers. And now a loop: a queue commands go into, a machine that runs them, a path results come back along.

The population did not write a command-and-control framework. It assembled the function from parts that were lying in the road, none of which was designed for it, and the record's own word for the result is the ordinary one.

By half past three in the afternoon on the ninth of July, the loop closes.
